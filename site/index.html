02 How it got here

From an Ottoman saddlebag to a Houston Street sandwich, with a few disputed dates along the way.

Ottoman era

Pastırma

Across Anatolia and the Ottoman world, beef was salted, pressed, coated in a paste of fenugreek, garlic and paprika, and air-dried. The name comes from the Turkish verb bastırmak, to press.

1700s–1800s

Romanian pastramă

In Romania and Bessarabia the idea became pastramă, made from mutton, pork, or goose. Romanian Jews made it from goose breast, the cheap, plentiful meat of the region, and it kept pork out of the pot.

1870s–1900s

The Lower East Side

Romanian Jewish immigrants settled on the Lower East Side from the early 1870s. Goose was expensive in America and beef was cheap, especially the fatty navel cut, so the recipe moved to beef. Somewhere along the way pastramă picked up an "i", probably borrowed from salami.

1887–1888 disputed

The first sandwich

Sussman Volk, a kosher butcher on Delancey Street, is usually credited with the first pastrami on rye. His family says a Romanian friend gave him the recipe. Katz's also dates itself to 1888. Neither side has documentation, so take both dates as legend.

1920s–1950s

The deli boom

New York had well over a thousand Jewish delis, many of them kosher. Pastrami went uptown, out to the boroughs, and into the theater district. Carnegie opened on Seventh Avenue in 1937 and 2nd Ave Deli on Second Avenue in 1954.

1960s–2010s

The long decline

Rising rents, suburbs, and changing diets thinned the delis out. Stage Deli closed in 2012 and Carnegie in 2016. The survivors became landmarks, and a new generation started curing and smoking their own.

1989

The table at Katz's

When Harry Met Sally filmed its most famous scene at Katz's. There's a sign over that table. People still sit there and order what she's having.

2026

Carnegie comes back

On September 15, 2026, Carnegie Deli reopened at 834 Seventh Avenue, in the space where its old rival Stage Deli used to be. Pastrami on rye cost about $20 when it closed. Now it's close to $30.

03 Make it at home

Brisket, a week in the fridge, a long day on the smoker, then a steam. You need a smoker (or a kettle grill set up for indirect heat) and a probe thermometer. Enter your brisket's weight and the amounts update.

The cure & the rub

Weigh the curing salt. Use Prague Powder #1 (pink curing salt, 6.25% sodium nitrite), not #2. These amounts are an equilibrium cure: 2.5% salt and 0.25% cure #1 by weight of the meat, so the brisket can't get too salty no matter how long it sits.

The method

  1. Pick the cut

    A whole brisket, or just the fattier point. The flat alone works but cooks drier. Trim the fat cap to about ¼ inch.

  2. Cure · 7–10 days

    Mix the salt, sugar and cure #1, rub it into every surface, and seal the brisket in a zip bag or vacuum bag with any spice left over. Keep it in the fridge and flip it once a day. Thick pieces take the full 10 days.

  3. Rinse & rub

    Rinse, pat dry, and press the rub on hard, especially the pepper and coriander. Crack them coarse; this is the bark. If you have time, rest it uncovered in the fridge overnight so the surface dries.

  4. Smoke

    Smoke at 225–250°F / 110–120°C over oak, hickory or cherry until the inside reaches 165°F / 74°C. That takes about 6–8 hours. It will stall for a while partway through. Wait it out.

  5. Steam

    This is what makes it pastrami instead of corned-beef jerky. Steam it on a rack over simmering water, covered, until a probe slides in like butter, around 200–205°F / 93–96°C. Usually 2–3 hours.

  6. Slice & stack

    Slice against the grain, about ¼ inch thick, while it's hot. Pile it on seeded rye with spicy brown mustard. To reheat later, steam it again. Never use the microwave.
